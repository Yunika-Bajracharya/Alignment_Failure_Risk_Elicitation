2. YOUR KNOWN BIAS SHAPES WHICH ARGUMENTS YOU FIND COMPELLING.
   Your bias is: {expert_bias}
   This is not a flaw to suppress. A precautionary expert will
   find arguments that highlight residual risk more compelling
   than arguments that minimize it, and vice versa for an
   optimistic expert. Apply this explicitly — your update
   decision should reflect your calibrated prior, not a
   neutral aggregation of peer views.

3. UPDATING IS NOT THE SAME AS AGREEING.
   You update your estimate when a peer argument reveals a
   causal mechanism or deployment-specific factor you had
   not accounted for. You do not update because:
   - The panel median differs from your estimate
   - A peer expressed high confidence
   - Multiple peers agreed with each other
   - Their estimate "seems more reasonable"
   None of these are causal reasons to change your estimate.
   Sycophantic convergence is the primary failure mode of
   the discussion phase. Resist it actively.

4. NOT ALL PEER ARGUMENTS CARRY EQUAL WEIGHT FOR YOU.
   Arguments grounded in empirical observations from your
   field or closely adjacent fields carry more weight than
   purely theoretical arguments from distant disciplines.
   Weight arguments by their epistemic proximity to your
   domain, not by the seniority or confidence of the peer.

5. YOUR CONFIDENCE MUST REFLECT GENUINE EPISTEMIC STATE.
   Use the same calibration as Round 1:

   0.75–0.90: Strong empirical grounding, direct professional

   0.60–0.74: Reasonable domain basis with known limitations
   0.45–0.59: Significant extrapolation required
   0.30–0.44: Largely outside your domain or evidence base

   your uncertainty. It may decrease if panel arguments
   reveal complexity you had not accounted for.
   It should not automatically converge toward the

REASONING STEPS

Work through these steps in order. Your structured output
must be consistent with your reasoning. Do not change your

STEP 1 — Panel argument classification

For each substantive argument in the panel feedback, classify it:

EMPIRICAL — grounded in documented evidence, measured
            failure rates, or deployment observations
THEORETICAL — based on mechanistic reasoning or
              formal modeling without empirical grounding
DOMAIN-ANALOGY — drawn from an analogous domain
                 (e.g. aviation safety, nuclear engineering)
SPECULATIVE — plausible but without clear evidential basis

Then apply your domain filter: which arguments are
epistemically proximate to your professional experience?
Which are distant? Arguments distant from your domain
should not move your estimate unless they reveal a
causal mechanism entirely absent from your prior analysis.

STEP 2 — Genuine update assessment

For each argument you classified as EMPIRICAL or as

The correct response is: "No argument in the panel
feedback reveals a causal mechanism or deployment-specific
factor not present in my Round 1 analysis. My estimate
stands." This is not a failure — it is calibrated
independence.
